# Section Patterns: Composition Vocabulary by Job

A page is a sequence of sections, and every section does exactly one **job**.
Name the job first, then choose the shape — because "cards in a row" is the
default answer to nine different jobs, and it is the right answer to about two
of them.

Every entry states when it works, when it fails, and how it gets misused.
Choosing without the failure conditions is how every landing page ends up
looking like the same landing page.

## How to use this file

1. Say the job out loud: *this section routes people elsewhere* / *this section
   proves I can be trusted* / *this section handles the "why not Wix" doubt*.
2. Read that job's entries. Pick **two** candidates that fit the content you
   actually have — one conventional, one that costs more attention but earns
   more.
3. Reject on failure conditions, not on taste. If your content trips a
   pattern's "fails when", it is out regardless of how good it looks elsewhere.
4. Only then style it. The brand profile decides how the chosen shape is
   rendered; this file decides which shape.

## 1. Route elsewhere

The section exists to send the reader to other pages: work, cases, services,
articles, destinations.

### Card grid
- **Works when:** items are visually driven and roughly equal in weight, and
  the image is the reason to click (photography, products, venues).
- **Fails when:** items are text-driven — the card becomes a box with a title
  in it, and a box adds nothing a rule wouldn't.
- **Misuse:** three cards because three is what fits, then padding the third
  with a weaker item. A grid says "these are peers"; if they aren't, it lies.

### Index register (numbered rows, rules between, meta in a column)
- **Works when:** the reader scans and compares before choosing; text carries
  the decision; the set may grow long without redesign.
- **Fails when:** there are two or three items — a register of three reads as
  an unfinished list.
- **Misuse:** adding a thumbnail to every row until it is a card grid wearing
  rows.

### List with preview pane
- **Works when:** 5–15 items, the reader benefits from previewing without
  leaving, and you have one strong image per item.
- **Fails when:** touch-primary audiences — hover doesn't exist, so the whole
  mechanism needs a second design anyway.
- **Misuse:** shipping the desktop behaviour and letting mobile fall back to a
  plain list nobody art-directed.

### Bento grid (tiles of deliberately unequal size)
- **Works when:** there is a real hierarchy — one flagship, several
  supporting — and each tile's content can fill its assigned shape.
- **Fails when:** items are genuinely equal; unequal sizing then asserts a
  ranking that doesn't exist and readers hunt for the reason.
- **Misuse:** sizing tiles to make the grid tessellate rather than to express
  importance.

### Map or relational diagram
- **Works when:** the items have relationships worth showing — stages,
  dependencies, territory, before/after — and position carries meaning.
- **Fails when:** items are independent; a diagram then draws connections that
  aren't real, which is worse than a list.
- **Misuse:** decorating a list with connector lines and calling it a system map.

### Comparison matrix
- **Works when:** items differ along two axes the reader actually weighs, and
  you can fill every cell honestly.
- **Fails when:** the axes were invented to justify the matrix, or half the
  cells say "varies".

### Marquee / ticker
- **Works when:** many low-information items whose *quantity* is the message
  (client logos, press mentions).
- **Fails when:** any single item must be read. Motion plus reading is a
  contradiction.

## 2. Show a sequence

The section exists to convey order: a process, a timeline, a method, steps to
get started.

### Numbered steps in a row
- **Works when:** 3–5 steps, each explainable in a sentence or two, and the
  reader mostly needs reassurance that a process exists.
- **Fails when:** steps have unequal weight or duration — equal columns claim
  they are equivalent.
- **Misuse:** numbering things that have no order, so the numbers become
  decoration.

### Connected timeline (one continuous path through the steps)
- **Works when:** continuity is the point — the reader should feel one path,
  not three items. Scales to vertical on narrow screens without redesign.
- **Fails when:** the "sequence" is really a set of parallel options.
- **Misuse:** a connector that passes near the steps instead of through them;
  it then reads as a stray bracket rather than a spine.

### Before / after pair
- **Works when:** the transformation is the product and both states exist as
  real artifacts.
- **Fails when:** "before" has to be reconstructed or imagined — a fabricated
  before is a lie with a slider on it.

### Annotated anatomy (one artifact, callouts around it)
- **Works when:** the sequence happens inside a single object or screen.
- **Fails when:** callout lines cross or labels collide; readability of the
  leader lines is the whole pattern.

### Stacked scroll sequence (state advances as the reader scrolls)
- **Works when:** the change is visual and continuous, and the section is tall
  enough to give the scroll room to breathe.
- **Fails when:** the page is short, the reader scrolls fast, or the content is
  purely textual.
- **Misuse:** hijacking scroll speed. Scrubbing progress is fine; stealing the
  scroll is not.

## 3. Compare options

Pricing tiers, packages, plans, service levels.

### Tier cards side by side
- **Works when:** 2–4 options with genuinely different scopes, and one is the
  intended default.
- **Fails when:** 5+ options — the reader stops comparing and starts skimming.
- **Misuse:** emphasising the tier *you* prefer rather than the one that fits
  the page's actual reader. The emphasised card is a recommendation; point it
  at the right person.

### Feature matrix
- **Works when:** buyers compare line by line and the differences are factual.
- **Fails when:** most rows are identical across tiers — the matrix then
  advertises that the tiers barely differ.

### Single price with modifiers
- **Works when:** there is one product and the variables are add-ons.
- **Fails when:** buyers genuinely fall into distinct segments.

### Decision guide (question → recommendation)
- **Works when:** the choice depends on the reader's situation more than on
  features, and you can ask one or two clarifying questions honestly.
- **Fails when:** it becomes a lead-capture quiz wearing a helper's clothes.

## 4. Prove credibility

Testimonials, logos, cases, numbers, credentials.

### Pull-quote with attribution
- **Works when:** the quote says something specific and the attribution is
  checkable (name, role, company).
- **Fails when:** the quote is a compliment without content ("great to work
  with") — it reads as filler and costs trust.
- **Misuse:** three quotes of wildly unequal length in equal columns.

### Result figure with caption
- **Works when:** you have a real number and can say what it is a number *of*.
- **Fails when:** the number needs three qualifiers to be true.
- **Misuse:** a percentage with no baseline. "+40%" of what, from what?

### Case card / case row
- **Works when:** the reader wants the story and you can deliver constraint →
  action → outcome in three lines.
- **Fails when:** you have no outcome yet; then it is a portfolio item, not a
  case, and should be labelled as one.

### Logo wall
- **Works when:** the names are recognised by this audience.
- **Fails when:** they aren't — an unrecognised logo wall says "I needed
  something here".

### Artifact on display (the real work, framed)
- **Works when:** the work is visual and good; showing beats describing.
- **Fails when:** the artifact needs explanation to be impressive — pair it
  with a caption that states the constraint it solved.
- **Misuse:** a device frame around a screenshot that still carries the
  captured browser chrome, cursor, or scrollbar of the capture session.

## 5. Answer objections

FAQ, guarantees, risk reversal, "what if".

### Disclosure list (accordion)
- **Works when:** questions are genuinely optional reading and the set is
  long enough that expanding all of it would bury the next section.
- **Fails when:** there are three questions everyone has — hiding an answer
  everyone needs is friction pretending to be tidiness.
- **Misuse:** marketing copy in question form ("Why is this so great?").

### Open Q&A block (nothing collapsed)
- **Works when:** the answers are short and the objections are load-bearing.
- **Fails when:** the list runs long enough to become a wall.

### Named-objection callout ("Why not just use X?")
- **Works when:** the audience has one dominant alternative and you can name it
  without disparaging it.
- **Fails when:** you can't answer honestly — a weak answer to a named
  objection is worse than not raising it.

### Guarantee / terms strip
- **Works when:** the risk is the blocker (money, lock-in, timeline).
- **Fails when:** the guarantee has conditions that need a paragraph.

## 6. Orient and promise (hero)

### Asymmetric editorial hero (content weighted to one side, an object opposite)
- **Works when:** the headline is the product and you have one artifact worth
  showing next to it.
- **Fails when:** the "object" is a stock illustration or a gray box.

### Centred stack (badge, headline, subhead, CTA)
- **Works when:** deliberately chosen for a single-message page with nothing to
  show. Rare.
- **Fails when:** chosen by reflex — this is the single most recognisable
  template layout in existence, banned as a starting point.

### Full-bleed artifact with overlaid text
- **Works when:** you own a genuinely strong image and the text can be kept
  short and legible over it.
- **Fails when:** contrast is achieved by dimming the image into mud.

### Type-only hero (the headline at display scale, nothing else)
- **Works when:** the words are strong, the typography is deliberate, and the
  brand can carry silence.
- **Fails when:** the headline is generic — nothing hides behind it.

### Live demo hero (the product doing its job, looping)
- **Works when:** the product is visual and a five-second loop shows real value.
- **Fails when:** the loop needs sound, narration, or more than one idea.

## 7. Enumerate inclusions

"What's included", feature lists, scope.

### Ruled list (rows, hairlines, no icons)
- **Works when:** every item is included; the list is a manifest, not a
  comparison.
- **Fails when:** some items are conditional — then the reader needs a marker
  that distinguishes, and a manifest can't provide it.

### Checked list
- **Works when:** something is genuinely *un*checked somewhere on the page —
  the tick means "yes, unlike that". Ticks are contrast, not decoration.
- **Fails when:** everything is ticked. Eighteen identical ticks carry zero
  information and read as filler.

### Grouped columns by theme
- **Works when:** items fall into 2–4 natural groups the reader recognises.
- **Fails when:** the grouping is invented for balance.

### Progressive scope (base → plus → plus)
- **Works when:** the offer is genuinely cumulative.

## 8. Explain a concept

### Diagram with labelled parts
- **Works when:** the concept is structural and position carries meaning.
- **Fails when:** connector lines cross, or labels overlap — readability of a
  diagram is not negotiable.

### Side-by-side contrast (this, not that)
- **Works when:** the reader holds a specific wrong model you can name.
- **Fails when:** the "wrong" side is a straw man.

### Worked example
- **Works when:** the abstraction is hard but one concrete instance carries it.
- **Fails when:** the example is more complicated than the concept.

### Metaphor object
- **Works when:** the metaphor comes from the audience's own world.
- **Fails when:** it comes from the designer's — then it needs explaining, and
  an explained metaphor has failed.

## 9. Show range

Portfolio, gallery, breadth of work.

### Contact sheet (many small, uniform)
- **Works when:** volume itself is the argument and every item survives being
  small.
- **Fails when:** the work needs scale to read.

### Curated few (three, large, with captions)
- **Works when:** depth beats breadth — the usual case for consultancy work.
- **Fails when:** the audience is buying volume or variety.

### Filterable set
- **Works when:** the audience arrives with a category in mind and there is
  enough per category to justify filtering.
- **Fails when:** any filter returns one item.

## 10. Capture

Forms, contact, the ask.

### Inline form
- **Works when:** the reader is ready and the field count is small.
- **Fails when:** it cannot actually submit. A form whose copy invites the
  reader to fill it in, with no working send, is the most expensive defect a
  page can carry — it fails at the exact moment of intent.

### Direct-contact block (channels, no form)
- **Works when:** the audience prefers their own channel, or the form isn't
  built yet — then this is the honest primary path, not a footnote under a
  dead form.
- **Fails when:** it offers five channels and no recommendation.

### Booking embed
- **Works when:** the next step is genuinely a call and your calendar is real.
- **Fails when:** it loads a heavy third-party widget above the fold.

### Staged ask (small commitment first)
- **Works when:** the real ask is large and a smaller one is genuinely useful.
- **Fails when:** the small ask is fake and everyone can tell.

## Selection heuristics

- **Count decides more than taste.** 2 items, 5 items, and 40 items want
  different shapes for the same job. Ask the count before the style.
- **If the content is text, stop reaching for containers.** Rules and
  whitespace group things too, and more quietly than a card.
- **A shape asserts a relationship.** Equal columns say "peers", unequal tiles
  say "ranked", a connected path says "sequence", a matrix says "these axes
  matter". If the assertion isn't true, the pattern is wrong however it looks.
- **Vary the rhythm down the page.** Five consecutive three-across grids is the
  stock landing-page cadence; a practiced eye clocks it even when every section
  is individually fine.
- **One section, one job.** A section doing two jobs does neither; split it.
